## 2.2 Global Cutoff

### Definition

In Global Cutoff, the island loses 100% of its bandwidth to the global internet, while the domestic network keeps working normally. The domestic network has three parts: the links between internet service providers (ISPs) on the island, the domestic internet exchange points (IXPs: TPIX, TWIX and STUIX), and locally hosted services. Global Cutoff completes the loss that 50% Degradation causes at half scale: bandwidth to the global internet falls to zero on every international cable.

Under Global Cutoff, a service works normally if its infrastructure is onshore and its dependencies use domestic routes. Any service with an offshore part or an offshore dependency becomes unreachable.

Global Cutoff does not mean the domestic network fails, so the report does not call it a "total blackout". When the domestic network also fails, the event is Island Partition, not Global Cutoff.

### What the failure looks like

Under Global Cutoff, the failure has a hard edge: international traffic stops instead of slowing. 50% Degradation produces a brownout, which is slow and lossy service: HTTP 504 gateway timeouts, higher packet loss, and congestion from Transmission Control Protocol (TCP) retransmission. Global Cutoff instead produces TCP connection timeouts for every destination outside Taiwan's domestic network.

A single connection attempt shows the hard edge: the connection request (a TCP SYN packet) gets no reply. The attempt then ends at the operating system's TCP timeout, not with a slow acknowledgment. Domain Name System (DNS) lookups show the same edge. A query for a non-.tw name whose authoritative servers are off-island gets SERVFAIL, not a late answer. SERVFAIL is the response code a resolver returns when it cannot complete a lookup.

Two different mechanisms have produced this hard edge: an administrative order and physical destruction. Iran's administrative shutdown of November 2019 worked by withdrawing Border Gateway Protocol (BGP) routes at the gateway autonomous systems, while the cables stayed physically intact. The shutdown kept data-plane connectivity (the actual carrying of traffic) low from November 16 until November 21 ([OONI, November 2019](https://ooni.org/post/2019-iran-internet-blackout/)). Tonga's cable loss of January 2022 produced the same edge through physical destruction. Afterward, Cloudflare Radar recorded "close to no traffic at all" from Tonga's ISPs ([Cloudflare Blog, Tonga Internet Outage, 2022](https://blog.cloudflare.com/tonga-internet-outage/)). In both events, the domestic network and domestically hosted services kept working while every foreign-hosted service stopped.

### Course over time

How fast a cutoff begins depends on the mechanism. An administrative shutdown has no brownout before it and, in network terms, begins at minute zero. This holds for both of its forms: gateway route withdrawal, and application-layer allowlisting, which passes only approved traffic. In Iran in 2019, cellular operators were disconnected first, at around 14:00 UTC on November 16, and almost all other providers followed over the next five hours ([OONI, November 2019](https://ooni.org/post/2019-iran-internet-blackout/)).

A physical cable cut is fast but not instant. BGP withdrawals spread within minutes of link loss. In Tonga, Cloudflare Radar recorded near-zero traffic within 2.5 hours of the eruption's impact ([Cloudflare Blog, Tonga Internet Outage, 2022](https://blog.cloudflare.com/tonga-internet-outage/)).

Recovery also depends on the mechanism. After physical damage, recovery follows an engineering schedule: a repair ship must be sent, and the cable must be spliced and tested. Tonga, a country with a single cable, reconnected its main island after 38 days, more than five weeks ([Al Jazeera, February 2022](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2022/2/23/tonga-internet-reconnected-five-weeks-of-volcanic-eruption/)). An administrative cutoff has no such limit. Nothing is physically broken, so no repair ship can end it. Restoration is a political act.

Documented administrative cutoffs at the gateway range from Iran's seven-day, protest-linked shutdown of 2019 ([OONI, November 2019](https://ooni.org/post/2019-iran-internet-blackout/)) to a later Iranian shutdown. The report classes such cutoffs as gateway failure kind k1, defined in §3.2.1. Al Jazeera described the later shutdown on April 5, 2026 as already record-breaking while it was still in force ([Al Jazeera, April 2026](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/4/5/frustration-grows-as-irans-wartime-internet-shutdown-breaks-grim-record/)). Cloudflare reported the same shutdown as partially restored in May 2026 ([Cloudflare Blog, Iran Internet Partially Restored, May 2026](https://blog.cloudflare.com/iran-internet-partially-restored-may-2026/)). *(Inference from OONI November 2019 and Al Jazeera April 2026 documented shutdown durations and Cloudflare May 2026 partial-restoration data; not independently confirmed)* Economic pressure played a part in both cases but did not reliably end either shutdown on any predictable schedule.

If the cutoff lasts, whatever its mechanism, a second Phase follows: items that have an expiry time reach it, each on its own schedule. Cached DNS records lapse first, each at the end of its time-to-live (TTL), the period for which a resolver may keep the record. Short-TTL records lapse within hours of onset, and records served through content delivery networks (CDNs) within a day. Medium-TTL platform records lapse within two to three days, and mail-exchanger (MX) and name-server (NS) records within a week.

Certificates and signatures also expire in this second Phase. Certificate renewal through offshore Automatic Certificate Management Environment (ACME) endpoints stops at onset, but each certificate fails only when it expires, days to weeks later. DNS Security Extensions (DNSSEC) signature (RRSIG) records fail as their signing windows close. Protocol-Clock Cascade simulates this delayed wave of failures.

### Recognition signatures

Global Cutoff appears in traffic data as a cliff: international traffic drops suddenly to near zero while domestic traffic continues. The cliff rules out 50% Degradation, where traffic declines on a gradual slope. The continuing domestic traffic rules out Island Partition, under which domestic traffic between the two halves of the island also fails.

BGP feeds, the public streams of routing updates, are not enough alone to detect the cliff. Three mechanisms produce the same traffic collapse with different routing behavior, and under two of them BGP feeds show nothing:

| Mechanism | BGP feeds | Collapse visible in | Recovery path |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cable cut (physical damage) | Simultaneous WITHDRAW messages arrive within minutes of link loss. Monitors raise an alert, and routing-table observers and RIPE Atlas probes confirm the withdrawal. | Cloudflare Radar shows a step drop toward near zero. | Physical repair queue, limited by ship dispatch and engineering work |
| Allowlisting (administrative order) | Routes stay announced, so monitors raise no alert. | Only active probing (IODA) and traffic volume (Cloudflare Radar) show it. | Political reversal, in stages set by policy |
| Forwarding failure inside a carrier | Routes stay advertised, so monitors raise no alert. | Active probing shows it, with a response pattern different from allowlisting. | Restored in geographic batches |

Administrative route withdrawal, as in Iran in 2019, shows the same signatures as the first row. It differs from a cable cut only in its recovery path, which is political reversal.

Each row rests on a documented incident. The cable-cut row rests on Tonga, where the BGP update spike was recorded at 05:35 UTC on January 15, 2022 ([Cloudflare Blog, Tonga Internet Outage, 2022](https://blog.cloudflare.com/tonga-internet-outage/)).

The allowlisting row rests on Iran's January 2026 shutdown. During that shutdown, 98.14% of Iranian IPv4 address space stayed globally routed throughout, yet human-generated traffic fell to well under 1% of previous levels ([arXiv preprint 2603.28753, 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.28753v1); [Cloudflare Blog, Iran Internet Partially Restored, May 2026](https://blog.cloudflare.com/iran-internet-partially-restored-may-2026/)). IODA (Internet Outage Detection and Analysis), an active-probing platform, showed approximately 3% nominal responsiveness. That responsiveness represented allowlisted access for government entities. An engineer watching only BGP feeds therefore saw no event, while users experienced a full cutoff.

The forwarding-failure row rests on Rogers Canada's July 2022 outage. Approximately 240 IPv4 and 120 IPv6 routes stayed advertised in Rogers' routing table while traffic fell to near zero. They stayed advertised for more than eight hours. The failure lay in the internal forwarding plane (the part of the network that moves packets), not in the routing protocol ([Kentik, A Deeper Dive into the Rogers Outage, 2022](https://www.kentik.com/blog/a-deeper-dive-into-the-rogers-outage/)). BGP feeds miss a forwarding failure as they miss allowlisting, but the cause is a failure, not a policy.

DNS failures begin at onset and then increase. When BGP withdrawal causes the cutoff, SERVFAIL rises at once for domains whose authoritative servers are off-island. SERVFAIL can rise earlier when application-layer filtering comes before withdrawal. In Iran in 2019, OONI data show filtering leading withdrawal by approximately one hour.

Meta's October 2021 outage (§3.3.1) documents the step from BGP to DNS. The prefixes that hosted Meta's DNS were withdrawn, and recursive resolvers at once returned SERVFAIL for its domains ([Cloudflare Blog, October 4, 2021](https://blog.cloudflare.com/october-2021-facebook-outage/)). Under Global Cutoff, cached records then lapse on the TTL schedule given in the Course over time subsection.

### Simulated scenarios

#### Administrative Cutoff

A simulated scenario under Global Cutoff: on government order, the gateway operator cuts international traffic to near zero, either by withdrawing routes or by application-layer allowlisting. This is gateway failure kind k1, defined in §3.2.1. The cables stay physically intact, and the domestic network keeps working normally.

**What happens.** The simulated event begins when a government order reaches the autonomous systems that carry Taiwan's international gateway traffic. In the route-withdrawal form, as in Iran in 2019, routing-table monitors raise an alert within hours. Cellular operators withdraw before fixed-line operators, which leaves a staggered signature in public BGP feed data ([OONI, November 2019](https://ooni.org/post/2019-iran-internet-blackout/)). In the allowlisting form, as in Iran in 2026, routes stay globally announced, and only active-probing platforms and traffic-volume curves reveal the cutoff ([arXiv preprint 2603.28753, 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.28753v1)).

Both forms end the same way: international connectivity is near zero over undamaged cables. Onshore services with no foreign-routed dependency keep working, and every foreign address is unreachable. Politics and economics, not an engineering schedule, decide how long the cutoff lasts.

**Precedent.** Iran's November 2019 shutdown is the precedent for the route-withdrawal form. Orders went to two state-controlled gateway entities, TIC (AS49666) and IPM (AS6736). The orders took effect at every ISP without any ISP having to act. Traffic fell by approximately 95% within hours while the cables were intact, and domestic banking, messaging and mapping services stayed fully operational throughout ([OONI, November 2019](https://ooni.org/post/2019-iran-internet-blackout/); [Kentik, From Stealth Blackout to Whitelisting, 2022](https://www.kentik.com/blog/from-stealth-blackout-to-whitelisting-inside-the-iranian-shutdown/)).

Iran's January 2026 shutdown is the precedent for the allowlisting form, at the same gateway infrastructure ([arXiv preprint 2603.28753, 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.28753v1)). An earlier measurement study found the same split between routing-plane and data-plane shutdowns in 2011. Egypt's shutdown appeared in BGP data as the withdrawal of its prefixes, and Libya appears to have tested firewall-based blocking before moving to BGP-based disconnection ([Dainotti et al., ACM IMC, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1145/2068816.2068818)).

**Applies to Taiwan:** partly. The mechanism applies, but the legal and political conditions differ. Taiwan's international bandwidth is concentrated in two Chunghwa Telecom gateway networks, HiNet and TWGate (§3.2.1). This is the gateway concentration that a coordinated order would require.

The political threshold is higher in Taiwan than under Iran's two-AS architecture. Taiwan has no sovereign-internet law and no standing authority for gateway route withdrawal comparable to the chain of orders from Iran's Ministry of ICT. An administrative shutdown would therefore require emergency legal powers over telecommunications and compliance by the carriers.

**How to tell it from All-Cable Loss.** In its allowlisting form, Administrative Cutoff differs from All-Cable Loss in BGP. Traffic collapses to near zero, but there is no withdrawal for routing-table monitors to see. In its route-withdrawal form, Administrative Cutoff shows the same withdrawal cliff as All-Cable Loss. The two then differ only in recovery, which comes from policy reversal, not from a physical repair queue.

**Drill note.** A tabletop exercise where a simulated gateway order collapses international traffic without BGP alerts would test whether teams detect it from active-probing dashboards, not routing-table monitors, and identify which services work on domestic routes alone.

#### All-Cable Loss

A simulated scenario under Global Cutoff: physical damage severs every international submarine cable, leaving zero cable bandwidth to the global internet while the domestic network operates normally.

**What happens.** The simulated event opens when Taiwan's international cables are cut, all at once or in a cascade. The cause is either coordinated physical damage or a submarine landslide like those the 2006 Hengchun earthquake set off (§2.1, Multi-Cable Loss). BGP withdrawals from the affected ASNs spread within minutes, and Cloudflare Radar records the cliff. Domestically hosted services keep working.

In a variant of this simulated scenario, the damage also destroys cable landing stations (the sites where submarine cables come ashore). The terminal infrastructure must then be rebuilt before cable repair can be completed. This variant shows the same BGP and traffic signatures as a cable-only cut, but the repair queue alone no longer sets its recovery. Recovery lengthens from weeks to months or longer, depending on which stations are destroyed and how much must be rebuilt.

**Precedent.** Tonga's January 2022 eruption, which cut the Tonga–Fiji cable, is the clearest available measurement of a country losing all of its cables. The subsections above give its onset signatures and the 38-day recovery of its main island ([Cloudflare Blog, Tonga Internet Outage, 2022](https://blog.cloudflare.com/tonga-internet-outage/); [Al Jazeera, February 2022](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2022/2/23/tonga-internet-reconnected-five-weeks-of-volcanic-eruption/)).

**Applies to Taiwan:** partly. Tonga had a single cable, whereas Taiwan had 15 international submarine cables at the end of February 2026 ([MODA press release, April 2026](https://moda.gov.tw/en/press/press-releases/19392)), with over 300 Tbps of designed capacity ([Ketagalan Media, February 2025](https://ketagalanmedia.com/2025/02/23/taiwans-submarine-cable-network-strategic-value-and-future-outlook/)). The 2006 Hengchun earthquake severed eight systems, and the cables that survived still prevented a complete cutoff. A complete cutoff would therefore need physical damage on a larger scale than Hengchun, or coordinated damage to every active cable system.

After Taiwan's earthquakes at the end of 2025, MODA rerouting absorbed a multi-cable event (§2.1, Multi-Cable Loss; [MODA press release, December 2025](https://moda.gov.tw/en/press/press-releases/18412); [MODA press release, April 2026](https://moda.gov.tw/en/press/press-releases/19392)). Rerouting can therefore absorb a moderate multi-cable loss, and it becomes insufficient only at some larger scale.

**How to tell it from Administrative Cutoff.** Two features separate All-Cable Loss from Administrative Cutoff. Unlike the allowlisting form of Administrative Cutoff, All-Cable Loss always opens with a BGP withdrawal cliff that routing-table monitors detect. Unlike the route-withdrawal form, All-Cable Loss recovers only through a physical repair queue that no policy reversal can shorten.

**Drill note.** A tabletop exercise that shows the Cloudflare Radar and IODA signatures of a simultaneous multi-cable cut would test whether teams triage the incident from BGP withdrawal feeds and traffic-cliff data and identify which domestically hosted services stay reachable when physical repair timelines rule out any near-term restoration of international cables.

#### Satellite Trickle

A simulated scenario under Global Cutoff: cable bandwidth is gone, and the remaining satellite capacity carries only critical-service traffic. That capacity is a trickle (a very small flow) to ration, not a network restored to general use.

**What happens.** Satellite Trickle begins inside another event under Global Cutoff, most naturally All-Cable Loss, once the remaining satellite paths are the only international link. Priority allocation sends the trickle to emergency coordination, government communications and prioritized financial transactions. Chunghwa Telecom prioritized critical circuits in a similar way on Matsu's microwave backup during the 2023 cable loss (§2.1; [Taipei Times, February 10, 2023](https://www.taipeitimes.com/News/taiwan/archives/2023/02/10/2003794094/)). General civilian internet traffic does not pass at a usable speed, if it passes at all.

Satellite capacity available over Taiwan is well under 0.1% of its cable bandwidth (§3.1.2). No measured figure for the share usable in a cutoff has been published.

One low-Earth-orbit (LEO) terminal delivers tens of Mbps. MODA reports average download speeds of up to 90 Mbps for the OneWeb service that its contingency network uses (§3.1.2). For Starlink, a different LEO system, crowdsourced measurements put most clients at about 50–100 Mbps download at the 75th percentile. The measurements cover 19.2 million speed tests from 34 countries since 2021 ([Mohan et al., ACM Web Conference, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1145/3589334.3645328)).

Taiwan's published plans describe the same rationing. Chunghwa Telecom has said that if all of Taiwan's more than ten international cables are cut, high-, mid- and low-orbit satellites take over in layers. The National Communications Commission's (NCC) wartime plan, as reported, cuts public 4G/5G capacity to 1% of normal and carries voice and SMS only (劉致昕, 管婺媛 and 陳泳丞, 〈戰火下的新商業〉, *商業周刊* no. 2029, October 5, 2026, pp. 84–85). The same report quotes 林一平 (Lin Yi-Bing) of National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University warning of signalling overload (「信令過載」). The report also notes that under disaster roaming a base station sized for 200 people serves 300.

Chunghwa Telecom also puts its prepared capacity at about 50–60 times (五、六十倍) what basic communication (「基本通訊」) needs (劉致昕, 管婺媛 and 陳泳丞, 〈戰火下的新商業〉, *商業周刊* no. 2029, October 5, 2026, p. 84). That multiple is measured against basic communication, not normal traffic, so it does not measure the usable share.

**Precedent.** After Tonga's cable was cut in January 2022, Cloudflare Radar recorded only "minimal Internet traffic via satellite services" from Tonga's ISPs. The signal, measurably above zero, came from individual satellite-terminal users, not from an activated national backup plan ([Cloudflare Blog, Tonga Internet Outage, 2022](https://blog.cloudflare.com/tonga-internet-outage/)).

Tonga signed a 15-year satellite connectivity agreement with Kacific in April 2019. The agreement had never been activated and was in arbitration in Singapore when the eruption cut the cable. Two days after the eruption, Kacific said it was still waiting for the government's instructions to activate the service ([ZDNet, January 17, 2022](https://www.zdnet.com/article/kacific-waiting-for-tongan-contract-activation-to-supply-satellite-services-post-eruption/)). *(Structural inference from the unactivated Kacific agreement)* A signed satellite contract is therefore not an operational backup until it is activated.

Ukraine's experience in 2022–24 supports calling satellite capacity a trickle rather than a network. Starlink's traffic share grew by over 1,600% from March to December 2022, and its daily users reached approximately 150,000 or more by January 2023. Yet Starlink never exceeded 0.3% of total Ukrainian internet traffic at any measured point. Its value lay in frontline and mission-critical uses, not in restoring traffic for the general population ([Cloudflare Blog, One Year of War in Ukraine, February 2023](https://blog.cloudflare.com/one-year-of-war-in-ukraine/)).

**Applies to Taiwan:** partly. Taiwan has no national satellite backup prepared in advance. LEO capacity over Taiwan is not sized as a national fallback at volumes close to normal cable volumes. Starlink is not lawfully available to civilian users onshore, although the July 2026 amendment removed the foreign-ownership barrier (§3.1.2; [Focus Taiwan, July 21, 2026](https://focustaiwan.tw/business/202607210011)).

**How to tell it from All-Cable Loss.** Satellite Trickle leaves a small but nonzero signal in Cloudflare Radar and active-probing data. Within that small signal, priority traffic survives in a larger share than general consumer traffic. All-Cable Loss with no remaining path instead collapses to near zero and leaves only noise-level signals.

**Drill note.** A tabletop exercise giving teams a simulated bandwidth budget in the trickle range would test how they rank traffic: which service types survive on severely limited international capacity, not how to restore full international connectivity.

#### Protocol-Clock Cascade

A simulated scenario under Global Cutoff: the cutoff lasts long enough for items with an expiry time to run out, producing a second, delayed wave of failures separate from those at onset. The items that expire include push tokens, refresh tokens, and software-license check-ins and similar check-ins. They also include Transport Layer Security (TLS) certificates, which can no longer be renewed because the ACME endpoints are offshore. DNSSEC signatures and Online Certificate Status Protocol (OCSP) responses expire as well.

**What happens.** The simulated event begins once the first Phase of Global Cutoff has settled and the services that survived the loss of connectivity appear to work. A second wave of failures then arrives hours to weeks later. Because each protocol layer has its own expiry time, the wave builds up over days and weeks.

Expiring certificates cannot renew, because the ACME endpoint is offshore. As RRSIG records reach the end of their signing windows, DNSSEC-validating resolvers return SERVFAIL for names that resolved before. OCSP staples (certificate-status responses that a server attaches) expire once a server's cached response passes the ten-day validity cap analyzed in §3.3.2 ([CA/Browser Forum Baseline Requirements 2.2.8, §4.9.9](https://cabforum.org/working-groups/server/baseline-requirements/documents/CA-Browser-Forum-TLS-BR-2.2.8.pdf)). After that, clients that enforce hard-fail OCSP checking reject certificates that are otherwise valid. Push tokens, software-license check-ins and refresh tokens expire at times set by each product.

**Precedent.** [IETF RFC 8555, March 2019](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8555) specifies an ACME renewal model in which the client must reach a certificate authority (CA) endpoint to complete each renewal cycle. [IETF RFC 4034, March 2005](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4034) and [IETF RFC 4035, March 2005](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4035) specify RRSIG validity windows that expire unless the authoritative infrastructure re-signs the records.

The expiry of Let's Encrypt's DST Root CA X3 root on September 30, 2021, shows certificate-lifecycle failure under partial isolation (§3.3.2; [Let's Encrypt, DST Root CA X3 Expiration, September 2021](https://letsencrypt.org/docs/dst-root-ca-x3-expiration-september-2021/)). The IANIX DNSSEC outage list records signature failures across multiple incidents. One is the May 2026 key-rollover failure in the German .de top-level domain (TLD), which sent SERVFAIL to all DNSSEC-validating resolvers for millions of .de domains. The .de failure shows how one failed validation step spreads to every resolution path that crosses it ([IANIX DNSSEC outage list](https://ianix.com/pub/dnssec-outages.html)).

*(Structural inference from RFC 8555 ACME renewal model, RFC 4034 and RFC 4035 DNSSEC RRSIG validity windows, and the Let's Encrypt DST Root CA X3 expiry documentation cited above)* A sustained national cutoff would combine these expiry windows into a single delayed wave. No national-cutoff incident of this combined form is documented, so this simulated scenario rests on mechanism, not on an observed incident.

**Applies to Taiwan:** in mechanism only. The mechanism applies strongly. Taiwan's commercial services rely heavily on Let's Encrypt and other offshore ACME-issuing certificate authorities for TLS certificates. DNSSEC signing for commercial .tw domains crosses offshore authoritative infrastructure. Government domains under .gov.tw, by contrast, operate under the Government Root Certification Authority (GRCA, operated by DGBAS). GRCA is an onshore certificate authority that keeps working under Global Cutoff.

**How to tell it from All-Cable Loss.** Protocol-Clock Cascade differs from All-Cable Loss in timing. All-Cable Loss fails at the moment the cables are cut, with TCP timeouts and immediate SERVFAIL for off-island names. Protocol-Clock Cascade fails days to weeks after onset, in a second wave after a period of stability. The second wave follows the schedule of certificate and DNSSEC expiry dates, not the moment of connectivity loss.

**Drill note.** A tabletop exercise that starts after the connectivity loss has stabilized and moves the exercise clock forward a few days at a time would test whether participants identify which protocol windows are expiring, which services are newly failing, and how each failure shows up, with no path back to international connectivity.
