## 2.3 Island Partition

### Definition

Island Partition is the worst case on the escalation ladder, because two failures happen together. The north–south domestic long-haul trunks (the main long-distance links inside Taiwan) are cut, so northern Taiwan cannot reach southern Taiwan. At the same time, all global connectivity is lost.

Island Partition inherits every effect of Global Cutoff in full. International traffic stops completely, and cache entries of the Domain Name System (DNS) for non-.tw names expire with no way to refresh them. Time-limited protocol data expires as well, and connections fail completely instead of slowing down.

The domestic split is added to this global loss. The brownout effects of 50% Degradation (slow, lossy service) are included and exceeded. Each half of the island, north or south, therefore becomes a working network that can reach no other network, domestic or international.

The definition requires both conditions in full: no global access and no north–south link. A partition in which some global access survives falls outside the definition, and the report analyzes that case only as the simulated scenario Partial-Access Partition.

### What the failure looks like

The domestic network inside each half works as it did before the event. Latency and packet loss are normal, and services hosted in that half are fully reachable. Across the boundary between the halves, the network fails completely: the failure is complete unreachability, not degradation.

A service in the other half is unreachable by the same mechanism as any foreign-hosted service under Global Cutoff. The connection request (the TCP SYN) gets no reply, and the attempt ends only when the operating system times out. The attempt neither fails quickly nor completes slowly.

The failure across the boundary separates Island Partition from the two lower levels. Under 50% Degradation, cross-island traffic is degraded but not zero, and under Global Cutoff the domestic network is fully operational. Under Island Partition, domestic traffic between the halves is as unreachable as international traffic.

The closest available real case is below national scale: the routing of occupied Kherson, Ukraine, in 2022–2024 (§2.1, Compromised Transit). In May 2022, Russian forces coerced KhersonTelecom (AS47598) onto upstream providers in Crimea (the networks that carry another network's traffic onward). Kentik then found, in data from route collectors (servers that record the routes that networks announce), that the city was isolated from Ukrainian networks ([Kentik, Rerouting of Kherson follows familiar gameplan, August 2022](https://www.kentik.com/blog/rerouting-of-kherson-follows-familiar-gameplan/)). RIPE Labs measured the matching reversal in latency direction ([RIPE Labs, Resilience of the Internet in Ukraine, One Year On, April 2023](https://labs.ripe.net/author/emileaben/the-resilience-of-the-internet-in-ukraine-one-year-on/)).

The Kherson case is similar in structure but is not a direct precedent. The case happened below national scale, and the unoccupied parts of Ukraine kept their global connectivity. No comparable real event documents what Island Partition requires: a partition at island scale combined with a global cutoff at the same time.

### Course over time

*(Structural inference from the Island Partition definition)* The partition appears in routing within minutes of the trunk cuts. Networks announce their routes to each other through the Border Gateway Protocol (BGP). In those minutes, the routes between the two halves (BGP prefixes) are withdrawn or silently dropped (black-holed).

*(Inference from the Ukraine/Kherson occupied-territory routing analysis, RIPE Labs and Kentik, 2022–2024; not independently confirmed)* For planning, this report assumes that a partition of Taiwan would take days to settle, a scale comparable to Kherson. Kherson had a city-wide outage on April 30, 2022. On May 1, the first network returned through the Crimean provider. After a further outage on May 30, Kherson's internet service providers (ISPs) lost their links to other Ukrainian networks, "possibly for good". Within a few days, every network in the city was either offline or connected through the Crimean provider ([Kentik, Rerouting of Kherson follows familiar gameplan, August 2022](https://www.kentik.com/blog/rerouting-of-kherson-follows-familiar-gameplan/)).

*(Structural inference from the Island Partition and Global Cutoff definitions)* In the first phase, from hours to days after onset, the processes of Global Cutoff run in both halves at once. Applications whose servers are in the other half time out as they do under Global Cutoff. DNS cache entries for names in the other half or abroad expire as their time-to-live periods (TTLs) end, with no way to refresh them.

*(Structural inference from the Island Partition and Global Cutoff definitions)* The protocol clocks (time limits built into protocols) were already counting down: TLS certificate renewal windows, OCSP stapling refresh, and DNSSEC RRSIG validity periods. In this first phase, these clocks approach the delayed failures described under Protocol-Clock Cascade.

*(Inference from the Ukraine/Kherson occupied-territory routing analysis, RIPE Labs and Kentik, 2022–2024; not independently confirmed)* In a second phase, a partition maintained by forced rerouting stays exposed to further disruption along the coerced upstream route. Forced rerouting means that an outside party coerces a network onto an upstream provider that the outside party chooses. Kherson's traffic was forced through Crimea in this way. In October 2022, damage to the Kerch Strait Bridge cable then raised latency to networks that depend on UMLC by 5–10 ms, as RIPE Labs documented ([RIPE Labs, Resilience of the Internet in Ukraine, One Year On, April 2023](https://labs.ripe.net/author/emileaben/the-resilience-of-the-internet-in-ukraine-one-year-on/)).

### Recognition signatures

**BGP and routing plane.** *(Structural inference from the Island Partition definition)* At onset, routes between the halves are withdrawn or black-holed, while routes within each half stay announced and stable. A BGP monitor or route collector inside either half therefore records no withdrawal events. External route collectors see the networks of each half (autonomous systems, identified by AS numbers or ASNs) become unreachable from the other half. This change shows in AS-path data, which lists the networks that each route crosses.

*(Structural inference from the Island Partition definition and the Kentik Kherson routing documentation cited above)* A partition produced by forced rerouting, not by route withdrawal, changes the AS path detectably. Within days of onset, prefixes from one half are announced through a new upstream AS, as at Kherson (§2.1, Compromised Transit; [Kentik, Rerouting of Kherson follows familiar gameplan, August 2022](https://www.kentik.com/blog/rerouting-of-kherson-follows-familiar-gameplan/)). Such a partition therefore has its own routing signature: no withdrawals between the halves, together with a new upstream AS path for one half. Physical connectivity survives but passes through an adversary's upstream, whereas in a partition produced by route withdrawal the paths between the halves disappear from the routing table.

**Traffic plane.** *(Structural inference from the Island Partition definition)* Within each half, traffic volume and loss rate stay at their pre-event levels. Traffic between the halves is zero, not lossy, and it fails by timeout, as international traffic does under Global Cutoff. RIPE Atlas probes are small measurement devices hosted on volunteers' networks. A probe in the northern half reaches northern services at normal latency and times out on every southern and international destination. A probe in the southern half shows the mirror image.

*(Structural inference from the Island Partition definition)* This two-way split, with normal traffic inside each half, is the definitive sign in the measurements that separates Island Partition from Global Cutoff. Under Global Cutoff, probes anywhere on the island see zero outbound connectivity, while domestic traffic flows across the whole island.

*(Inference from the Ukraine/Kherson occupied-territory routing analysis, RIPE Labs and Kentik, 2022–2024; not independently confirmed)* Aggregate traffic on Cloudflare Radar (Cloudflare's public traffic dashboard) cannot separate the two levels alone. Both halves have lost global access, so the aggregate under Island Partition itself falls to near zero, much like the sudden drop under Global Cutoff. Separating the two levels on Cloudflare Radar requires city-level data. In that data, some cities show domestic traffic at pre-event levels alongside zero international traffic. This pattern is absent under Global Cutoff, where all cities show near-zero outbound traffic.

**DNS and cache behavior.** *(Structural inference from the Island Partition and Global Cutoff definitions)* Within each half, names hosted abroad or in the other half resolve as they do under Global Cutoff. Cache entries expire as their TTLs end, with no way to refresh them. A resolver returns SERVFAIL (the response code for a lookup that it cannot complete) for any name whose authoritative server is off the island or in the other half. An authoritative server holds the official records of a name.

*(Structural inference from the Island Partition definition)* A .tw zone whose primary authoritative nameservers are in the other half depends on secondary nameservers in the user's own half. These secondary nameservers are the only mechanism that prevents split-brain SERVFAIL, a lookup failure caused by the split between the halves. Without them, the connectivity failure becomes a name-resolution failure for users in that half. The name-resolution failure occurs even when the domain's web servers are in that half and operational.

**Recovery path.** *(Structural inference from the Island Partition definition)* Recovery requires two failures to be resolved independently. The cut trunks follow the timeline of a physical engineering repair. The loss of global connectivity follows the timeline of a cable repair or of an administrative reversal. Neither repair resolves the other, so restoration takes at least as long as the longer timeline. The two independent repairs give Island Partition the compound worst-case recovery on the escalation ladder.

*(Inference from the Ukraine/Kherson occupied-territory routing analysis, RIPE Labs and Kentik, 2022–2024; not independently confirmed)* Where forced rerouting maintains the partition, restoring the pre-partition path requires removing the outside coercion. Removing the coercion is a political or military event, not a cable splice. RIPE Labs reported that latency at Kherson returned to pre-partition levels "pretty quickly" once liberation on November 11, 2022 removed the coercion. This return of latency is consistent with routing going back to its pre-partition paths as soon as the AS-path coercion ended ([RIPE Labs, Resilience of the Internet in Ukraine, One Year On, April 2023](https://labs.ripe.net/author/emileaben/the-resilience-of-the-internet-in-ukraine-one-year-on/)).

**Telling the simulated scenarios apart.** *(Structural inference from the Island Partition and Partial-Access Partition definitions)* Partial-Access Partition differs from Island Partition itself in traffic volume and in its geographic distribution. Island Partition itself shows near-zero aggregate traffic from both halves on Cloudflare Radar and in the active probing of IODA (the Internet Outage Detection and Analysis project). Partial-Access Partition shows a drop to a lower level, not a fall to near zero, and residual traffic from one half makes city-level data geographically uneven.

*(Structural inference from the Island Partition and Single-City Isolation definitions)* Single-City Isolation differs in the geographic scope of the traffic failure. Under Island Partition itself, RIPE Atlas probes anywhere in either half reach nothing in the other half and nothing abroad. Under Single-City Isolation, probes outside the isolated city show no partition anomaly, and a probe inside the city reaches no destination outside the city.

*(Structural inference from the Island Partition and Site-Level Blackout definitions)* Site-Level Blackout produces no BGP signal and requires no island-wide routing event. A site with no upstream connectivity takes no part in the routing table. In measurements, the site cannot be told apart from a network segment that is switched off, and the site shows no split in reachability. The site's absence from the responses to active probing is therefore the only sign visible from outside.

### Simulated scenarios

#### Partial-Access Partition

A simulated scenario under Island Partition: the north–south domestic trunks are cut and most global connectivity is lost. One half keeps a surviving international cable path, which gives limited global access to that half only. The other half has none.

**What happens.** The two halves fail unevenly. The half with no surviving cable follows Island Partition itself: it reaches neither the other half nor the rest of the world. In the other half, a cable landing station (the site where a submarine cable comes ashore) survives in one geographic cluster, such as the southern cluster at Fangshan (枋山). The surviving station provides a small amount of global bandwidth.

*(Structural inference from the k1, k2, and k3 gateway-kind descriptions in §3.2.1 and the Island Partition definition)* Whether that bandwidth is usable depends on the international gateway that serves the surviving cable. The table gives the result for each failure kind of that gateway:

| Gateway kind (§3.2.1) | Global access of the half that keeps a cable |
|---|---|
| k1, administrative shutdown | None in practice. The gateway blocks all outbound traffic, whatever physical path it arrives on. The physical survival of the cable therefore makes no practical difference |
| k2, throughput ceiling | Like a brownout. An administrative throttle on general civilian traffic leaves the surviving half's international access closer to what 50% Degradation looks like. The other half has no global access |
| k3, forced rerouting | Traffic flows, but it passes through adversary-controlled infrastructure. This exposes the half's international communications to content inspection and forced path selection |

Under every gateway kind, domestic traffic between the halves stays unreachable. The cut north–south trunks are the defining failure of Island Partition, and they carry no traffic whatever the gateway policy.

**Precedent.** *(Inference from the Ukraine/Kherson occupied-territory routing analysis, RIPE Labs and Kentik, 2022–2024; not independently confirmed)* The Kherson case documents uneven access below national scale. The city received limited connectivity through Crimea, while unoccupied Ukrainian regions kept their own international paths. Two geographically distinct areas with different upstream providers therefore operated at the same time. Two sources document how the routing paths and the latency direction of the two areas differed: [Kentik, Rerouting of Kherson follows familiar gameplan, August 2022](https://www.kentik.com/blog/rerouting-of-kherson-follows-familiar-gameplan/) and [RIPE Labs, Resilience of the Internet in Ukraine, One Year On, April 2023](https://labs.ripe.net/author/emileaben/the-resilience-of-the-internet-in-ukraine-one-year-on/).

*(Inference from the dComms roundtable, June 2026; not independently confirmed)* The dComms roundtable identified the gateway failure kind (k1, k2, or k3) as what determines whether a surviving physical cable gives usable global access.

**Applies to Taiwan:** partly. The Kherson case is a comparable event of uneven access, but the scale differs: Kherson was below national scale, and this scenario covers the whole island. Taiwan's southern landing stations at Fangshan (枋山) are geographically separated from the northern stations. The physical survival of one station cluster without the other is therefore plausible under the physical triggers that produce Island Partition.

**How to tell it from Single-City Isolation.** *(Structural inference from the Partial-Access Partition and Single-City Isolation definitions)* The difference is geographic scope. Partial-Access Partition cuts the north–south trunks across the whole island and leaves the halves with unequal international access. Single-City Isolation cuts all the upstream links of one municipality, while the domestic network in the rest of each half stays intact.

**Drill note.** A tabletop exercise for this scenario would change the surviving half's gateway failure kind from run to run (k1 shut down by administrative order, k2 throttled, k3 watched by an adversary, or open) and ask cross-functional teams, with no network information beyond the scenario, which services and communication paths survive under each gateway condition.

#### Single-City Isolation

A simulated scenario under Island Partition: one city (a municipality or urban area) loses all its upstream links to the rest of the island's domestic network at once. The wider island network keeps operating within each half of the partition.

**What happens.** A single physical event cuts every upstream link into the city. The event is a trunk failure along a geographic corridor, or cable damage at a bridge or mountain pass. The event can also be the simultaneous loss of all the last-mile aggregation points that feed the city (the sites where the final links to users join the wider network). Elsewhere in each half, the domestic network keeps operating normally.

From inside the city, every service hosted outside the city is unreachable, including services on the rest of the island. The north–south partition already divides those services, and the failure of the city's own upstream links now removes them entirely. The unreachable services also include international services, which the concurrent Global Cutoff already removes.

Services hosted inside the city still serve local users over the city's internal network, so emergency responders in the city can reach only local resources. Coordination systems, databases and communication paths hosted anywhere outside the city are as unreachable as foreign ones.

**Precedent.** *(Inference from the Ukraine/Kherson occupied-territory routing analysis, RIPE Labs and Kentik, 2022–2024; not independently confirmed)* The Kherson case documents routing isolation at city scale. All of the city's internet access was rerouted through a single non-Ukrainian upstream, and the rerouting made the city a routing zone disconnected from the Ukrainian domestic network. Kentik documents the BGP path change that produced this isolation ([Kentik, Rerouting of Kherson follows familiar gameplan, August 2022](https://www.kentik.com/blog/rerouting-of-kherson-follows-familiar-gameplan/)).

*(Inference from the dComms roundtable, June 2026; not independently confirmed)* The dComms roundtable identified Single-City Isolation as a scenario relevant to drills. The drills are for civil-society and private-sector engineers who reason about how local services survive without coordination from outside the city.

**Applies to Taiwan:** partly. The Kherson case is a comparable event at city scale, but the conditions differ: Kherson was isolated by forced rerouting, and this scenario by a physical cut. The scenario requires only the simultaneous cutting of a city's upstream long-haul fiber routes, which typically enter through a small number of trunk corridors and terrain chokepoints. Such a cut is structurally plausible under the physical triggers that produce Island Partition.

**How to tell it from Partial-Access Partition.** *(Structural inference from the Partial-Access Partition and Single-City Isolation definitions)* The difference is the extent of the domestic failure: Single-City Isolation does not require island-wide cutting of the north–south trunks. Single-City Isolation can occur while domestic connectivity is intact in every non-isolated part of each half, so its failure is confined to one municipality.

**Drill note.** A tabletop exercise for this scenario would limit participants to the resources and communication paths physically inside one city and ask teams which locally hosted services, local coordination mechanisms and local human and organizational channels still work when network access outside the city is zero.

#### Site-Level Blackout

A simulated scenario under Island Partition: a single rescue, shelter or critical-infrastructure site has no upstream connectivity at all, because its last-mile links are completely cut. The site becomes an isolated on-site network with no path to any external node on the domestic network or the global internet.

**What happens.** The site's last-mile connections to all its upstream providers are cut at once. The cause is cut fiber, destroyed antenna installations, or loss of the power supply that sustains its radio links. The site's internal network keeps operating: devices reach each other, and locally hosted services and locally cached data stay accessible. No packet crosses the site boundary in either direction.

Every digital resource that the site depended on but did not host is unreachable. Examples are patient records, authorization systems, real-time operational data and software updates. Staff cannot reach emergency coordination systems hosted elsewhere, verify instructions from outside authorities, send status reports, or reach any live data source outside the site. The site's only working network resources are those that were present and operational on site before the last-mile link failed.

**Precedent.** *(Structural inference from the Island Partition definition and the site-connectivity premise of the simulated scenario)* Site-Level Blackout follows from the definition of Island Partition: the partition and the global cutoff together cut every path out of each half. A site whose last-mile links are also cut then has no path to any external node, by construction.

No primary source documents an event at exactly this scale. That scale is a single rescue or shelter site with its last-mile links completely cut while Island Partition is in effect at the same time. The precedent therefore rests on mechanism, not on an incident.

The war in Ukraine offers a partial illustration at a comparable scale. Individual Starlink terminals (satellite internet terminals) were the only connectivity path for some frontline sites. The loss or suppression of those terminals left those sites with no electronic communication path to any outside node ([Cloudflare Blog, One Year of War in Ukraine, February 2023](https://blog.cloudflare.com/one-year-of-war-in-ukraine/)).

**Applies to Taiwan:** directly. Any critical site in Taiwan that depends on a single last-mile connection path is susceptible to this pattern under the physical conditions that produce Island Partition.

**How to tell it from Single-City Isolation.** *(Structural inference from the Island Partition and Site-Level Blackout definitions)* The difference lies in BGP and routing. Single-City Isolation produces a routing event on the city's upstream AS paths, which route-collector data shows. Single-City Isolation also produces zero reachability from inside the city's address space, which probes show. Site-Level Blackout produces neither, because a site with no upstream connectivity takes no part in the routing table.

**Drill note.** A tabletop exercise for this scenario would give responders only locally cached data and local communication paths, with no outside network access, and ask which operational decisions they can make from local resources alone, which need outside coordination they cannot reach, and which resources would have to be on site before the last-mile link failed.
