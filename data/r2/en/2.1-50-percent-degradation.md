## 2.1 50% Degradation

### Definition

50% Degradation is the first level of the escalation ladder. The whole island loses approximately 50% of its bandwidth to the global internet, and the domestic network stays intact. The domestic network means users, internet service providers (ISPs), the domestic internet exchange points (IXPs: TPIX, TWIX, STUIX), and services hosted in Taiwan. The report calls the network's behavior at this level brownout (slow, lossy service), and keeps the name 50% Degradation for the level itself.

No level lies below 50% Degradation, so 50% Degradation inherits no effects. Every effect in this section also occurs under Global Cutoff and Island Partition. The reverse does not hold. 50% Degradation includes neither the complete loss of international traffic that defines Global Cutoff nor the north–south domestic split that Island Partition adds.

### What the failure looks like

Under brownout, capacity is reduced rather than cut. Most connections still succeed, but slowly and with packet loss, and the slowest few fail outright. Only international paths are congested: the domestic network is intact, so traffic within Taiwan flows normally. This contrast between congested international paths and normal domestic traffic is itself a diagnostic sign.

Applications fail by timeout rather than by refusal. HTTP requests stall and either complete late or time out (HTTP 504, not 502 or 503). TCP sessions survive but run slowly, video pauses to buffer, text messages arrive minutes late, and voice calls drop intermittently.

The timeout pattern separates 50% Degradation from Global Cutoff and from normal conditions. Under Global Cutoff, connection requests (TCP SYNs) get no reply, and connection attempts end at the operating system's hard timeout. Under normal conditions, requests complete promptly.

Services fail in order of their need for low latency and high throughput. Streaming video, voice and real-time synchronization fail first. Text messaging and other protocols that exchange short text and acknowledgements survive longest. Priority traffic, such as banking, government and hospital communications, can be protected on the remaining capacity. That protection requires a quality-of-service (QoS) policy, a set of rules that gives chosen traffic priority.

The Matsu 2023 event followed this order. Both of Matsu's cables were cut, reducing capacity by approximately 75%, to roughly 25% of normal throughput. A text message could take 15 to 20 minutes to send, voice calls dropped intermittently, and Netflix and Instagram became unwatchable. Chunghwa Telecom reserved its microwave backup mainly for telephone, national-security, government, aviation and banking circuits, and gave government agencies, military units and hospitals preferential bandwidth. The Ministry of Digital Affairs (MODA) offered additional frequency bands to expand the relay ([Taipei Times, February 10, 2023](https://www.taipeitimes.com/News/taiwan/archives/2023/02/10/2003794094); [Taipei Times, February 17, 2023](https://www.taipeitimes.com/News/taiwan/archives/2023/02/17/2003794526); [Central News Agency via TechNews, February 16, 2023](https://technews.tw/2023/02/16/tw-matsu-cable-cht/); [The Wire China, June 2023](https://www.thewirechina.com/2023/06/11/cable-cutters-matsu-taiwan-china-undersea-internet-cables/); [The Diplomat, April 2023](https://thediplomat.com/2023/04/after-chinese-vessels-cut-matsu-internet-cables-taiwan-shows-its-communications-resilience/)).

When first switched on, the microwave backup provided 2.2 Gbps, against a normal cable capacity of 8–9 Gbps ([MODA press release, February 17, 2025](https://moda.gov.tw/en/press/press-releases/15210); [Domino Theory, Taiwan Internet Cable Problems](https://dominotheory.com/taiwans-internet-cable-problems-run-deep/)). The backup therefore started at about a quarter of the normal cable capacity.

### Course over time

A cable cut reduces international bandwidth within seconds to minutes of the physical break, so onset is fast. Under Multi-Cable Loss, a temporary reconvergence follows: routers settle on the surviving paths, using the Border Gateway Protocol (BGP). BGP is the protocol that networks use to announce routes to each other. Measurements of inter-domain routing have found that this reconvergence can take up to tens of minutes, with intermittent loss and high latency until it completes ([Labovitz et al., ACM SIGCOMM, 2000](https://doi.org/10.1145/347059.347428)).

*(Structural inference from the Matsu 2023 capacity ratio of approximately 25% of normal and the application-failure-order pattern documented under What the failure looks like above)* In the first phase after onset, demand from the whole island fills the surviving international bandwidth at once, and the failure order begins. Streaming and voice fail as each user's throughput falls below encoding and jitter thresholds. Priority allocation, if used, takes effect within the same phase.

Capacity then stays low, on a plateau, until the cause is removed. In the Matsu 2023 event the plateau lasted 51 days, more than seven weeks. The plateau ran from the second cable cut, on approximately February 8, 2023, to the first partial restoration, on March 31, 2023. Microwave capacity grew in steps: to 3.8 Gbps by March 6, 2023, to the capacity of the cables by the end of 2023, and to 12.6 Gbps by January 2025. The last figure is above the weekday peak demand of 9.5 Gbps ([Taipei Times, March 8, 2023](https://www.taipeitimes.com/News/taiwan/archives/2023/03/08/2003795708); [MODA press release, February 17, 2025](https://moda.gov.tw/en/press/press-releases/15210)).

When cables are physically damaged, the queue for repair ships sets the length of the plateau. *(Structural inference from TeleGeography cable repair fleet size data)* The global fleet has approximately 60 vessels, and fewer than 20 of them are dedicated solely to repair. The fleet handles approximately 150–200 cable faults a year. A simultaneous multi-cable event in Taiwan therefore joins a queue shared with every other cable in the world ([Scientific American, citing TeleGeography fleet data](https://www.scientificamerican.com/article/iran-threats-expose-the-aging-fleet-that-repairs-undersea-internet-cables/)). Access restrictions can add months beyond the technical minimum. In the Red Sea in 2024, repair vessels needed permits, negotiated with Houthi-faction and Yemeni government actors, before they could enter the repair zone ([GeoCables, 2024](https://geocables.com/research/red-sea-cable-cuts-2024); [Marine Insight, Yemen political delay, 2024](https://www.marineinsight.com/shipping-news/yemens-political-disputes-delay-full-repairs-of-3-submarine-internet-cables-in-the-red-sea/)). Damage to cables on Taiwan's eastern seabed, in contested waters, could meet the same delay. A Gateway Throttle or Compromised Transit plateau involves no physical damage, so the repair queue does not limit it.

### Recognition signatures

**BGP and routing plane.** *(Structural inference from RFC 4271 route-withdrawal mechanics and the level's definition as capacity-constrained rather than route-severing)* Routers send no WITHDRAW messages (BGP messages that tell neighboring routers a route is no longer available) during the brownout plateau. None is sent because the surviving routes stay in the routing table: the bottleneck is physical capacity, not reachability ([IETF RFC 4271, January 2006](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4271)). BGP update volume over the plateau stays flat, or rises slightly as links at the network edge go down and come back. The volume shows no spike of several standard deviations, the kind that marks the onset of Global Cutoff. Monitoring tools that watch only BGP therefore raise no alert.

*(Structural inference from RFC 4271 route-withdrawal mechanics and the level's definition as capacity-constrained rather than route-severing)* The main sign of 50% Degradation is therefore a silent control plane (routing) together with a degraded data plane (traffic). The degraded data plane shows higher round-trip time (RTT), higher packet loss and lower throughput. This pairing separates 50% Degradation from Global Cutoff, where both planes signal disruption, and from normal conditions, where neither plane does.

No BGP event record was published for Matsu 2023. The Open Observatory of Network Interference (OONI) does not probe Taiwan's link to its outlying island of Matsu. The reason is OONI's focus: international censorship and accessibility, not the quality of inter-island cables. The active probing of Internet Outage Detection and Analysis (IODA) targets international reachability, not domestic cables within Taiwan ([OONI methodology, ooni.org/docs/methodology]; [IODA platform scope, ioda.inetintel.cc.gatech.edu]).

**Traffic plane.** *(Structural inference from the level's capacity-constrained definition and the Matsu 2023 measured capacity ratio of approximately 25% of normal)* Cloudflare Radar shows traffic below normal for the whole degraded period: traffic slopes down, stays on a plateau, then slopes up. Cloudflare Radar does not show the near-vertical drop to near zero that marks the onset of Global Cutoff. IODA's active probes to the affected address space keep getting replies, with higher RTT and higher loss.

*(Structural inference from TCP congestion-window mechanics per RFC 5681 and the level's capacity-constrained definition)* Delivered throughput falls even below the share of capacity that survives, because of how TCP reacts to loss. Sustained packet loss makes TCP shrink its congestion window, the amount of data that a sender may have in transit. TCP shrinks the window in both slow start and congestion avoidance ([IETF RFC 5681, September 2009](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc5681)).

**DNS and cache behavior.** *(Structural inference from RFC 1034 recursive-resolver timeout mechanics and the level's capacity-constrained definition)* Name resolution degrades gradually rather than all at once. The rate of SERVFAIL responses (the code a resolver returns when it cannot complete a lookup) rises as resolver RTT exceeds client timeouts. The rate of NXDOMAIN responses (the code for a name that does not exist) does not change, because authoritative servers stay reachable, if slowly. The cache hit rate may rise as clients retry local caches, and this rise partly hides the degradation. Online Certificate Status Protocol (OCSP) stapling, in which a server attaches a recent proof that its certificate is valid, typically succeeds where staples were refreshed shortly before onset. Transport Layer Security (TLS) handshakes mostly complete, but slowly, and the slowest handshakes (the p99 tail) fail outright ([IETF RFC 1034, November 1987](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc1034)).

Controlled experiments by Moura et al. support this gradual pattern. With 50% packet loss at the authoritative servers, caching and retries still let almost all clients resolve names, at the cost of higher latency for the slowest lookups ([Moura et al., ACM IMC, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1145/3278532.3278534)).

**Recovery path.** Recovery depends on the cause. Under Multi-Cable Loss, capacity returns step by step as each segment is repaired, at weeks per segment. Under Gateway Throttle, the cables stay intact throughout, and recovery is a policy decision with no engineering schedule. Under Compromised Transit, recovery requires a change in who controls routing upstream. Neither cable repair nor a policy reversal at Taiwan's gateways resolves Compromised Transit.

**Telling the simulated scenarios apart.** Two routing signals separate the three simulated scenarios of this level: WITHDRAW messages at onset, and changes in the autonomous system (AS) path. The AS path is the list of networks that a route crosses, each identified by an AS number (ASN). Route collectors such as RIPE RIS and Routeviews, public services that store the BGP messages they receive from many networks, record both signals.

| | Multi-Cable Loss | Gateway Throttle | Compromised Transit |
|---|---|---|---|
| WITHDRAW at onset | A brief spike, then silence once routes settle on surviving paths | None: only traffic classification changes | None: coerced rerouting substitutes a new upstream AS without withdrawing routes |
| AS-path change | None: surviving routes keep their original peering relationships | None | Transit ASNs controlled by the adversary replace neutral carrier ASNs |

### Simulated scenarios

#### Multi-Cable Loss

A simulated scenario under 50% Degradation: one physical event cuts several international cable systems at once. The surviving cables carry the load in brownout. The global queue for repair ships lengthens restoration far beyond the time needed to repair one segment.

**What happens.** An earthquake, a submarine landslide or coordinated damage cuts several cable systems in quick succession. The surviving cables carry the rerouted traffic at lower total bandwidth, and every user who shares the remaining capacity experiences brownout.

Landing-station loss with partial reroute is a variant of this simulated scenario. Physical damage takes one or more cable landing stations (the sites where submarine cables come ashore) out of service while the others keep operating. The cables at the surviving stations take over the lost traffic. The depth of the brownout is proportional to the share of traffic that had passed through the damaged station.

**Precedent.** The Hengchun earthquake of December 26, 2006 consisted of two shocks, of M7.0 and M6.9, off southern Taiwan. The shocks set off submarine landslides that cut eight cable systems: APCN, APCN-2, C2C, China-US CN, EAC, FLAG FEA, FNAL/RNAL, and SMW3. Repair took 11 ships and 49 days, or seven weeks ([Submarine Networks, December 2006](https://www.submarinenetworks.com/en/nv/news/cables-cut-after-taiwan-earthquake-2006)).

At the end of 2025, earthquake damage off eastern Taiwan disrupted six international cables: SJC2, PLCN, EAC1, FNAL, RNAL, and EAC2. MODA reported that all operators had finished rerouting traffic, and described external communication as stable, with possible slight delays at peak hours ([MODA press release, December 2025](https://moda.gov.tw/en/press/press-releases/18412); [MODA press release, April 2026](https://moda.gov.tw/en/press/press-releases/19392)).

**Applies to Taiwan:** directly. Hengchun shows worst-case simultaneous damage on the main island. The end-2025 response shows MODA's rerouting working on today's cables in a multi-cable event.

**How to tell it from Gateway Throttle.** Multi-Cable Loss shows BGP activity at onset: route collectors record a brief spike of WITHDRAW messages. Routers withdraw the routes through the cut cables, traffic reconverges onto surviving paths, and BGP is then silent. Gateway Throttle produces no BGP updates at any phase, because only the traffic-classification policy changes. That policy operates at the deep-packet-inspection (DPI) layer, where equipment classifies traffic by inspecting packet contents.

**Drill note.** A tabletop exercise would test whether teams recognize brownout and tell it apart from the onset of Global Cutoff, given three inputs: a scripted log of BGP reconvergence, a ratio of surviving capacity based on the eight cable systems lost at Hengchun, and a repair timeline lengthened by permit delays.

#### Gateway Throttle

A simulated scenario under 50% Degradation: the cables are intact, but international gateways throttle (deliberately slow) general traffic on an administrative order (gateway failure kind k2, defined in §3.2.1). The slowdown is the same across all carriers, and it shows no sign of a cable fault or of route withdrawal.

**What happens.** A government or carrier operator instructs gateway routers to cap general civilian traffic at a set throughput, using DPI to classify the traffic. Designated critical services, or services on an allowlist, keep their bandwidth. Users and applications cannot tell this brownout from the brownout that a cable loss causes.

**Precedent.** On March 10, 2021, Roskomnadzor, Russia's communications regulator, ordered Russian ISPs to throttle Twitter through TSPU DPI hardware. Censored Planet, probing actively from inside Russia, independently measured a ceiling of 130–150 kbps. The ceiling applied to 100% of mobile devices and 50% of fixed-line devices. The throttle lasted on mobile networks through at least May 2021. The peer-reviewed IMC 2021 paper calls the throttle "the first ever use of large-scale, targeted throttling for censorship purposes" ([Censored Planet / IMC 2021](https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3487552.3487858); [Access Now tracker](https://www.accessnow.org/russia-throttled-twitter/)).

**Applies to Taiwan:** partly. Taiwan has no sovereign-internet infrastructure and no hardware equivalent to TSPU in place. The mechanism would therefore need emergency regulatory authorization and DPI hardware at carrier level, which is standard equipment in major ISPs. Gateway Throttle is based on demonstrated technical feasibility, not on an institutional precedent from a regulatory environment like Taiwan's.

**How to tell it from Compromised Transit.** Gateway Throttle leaves the BGP AS path unchanged, because the throttle acts on traffic classification, which BGP route collectors cannot see. Under Compromised Transit, route collectors show a changed AS path on the affected international routes: transit ASNs controlled by the adversary replace neutral carrier ASNs.

**Drill note.** A tabletop exercise would test whether teams from carrier operations and product engineering, given traffic metrics filtered by DPI and no BGP alerts, recognize a brownout caused by gateway policy (gateway failure kind k2) and tell it apart from the onset of a cable loss without routing evidence.

#### Compromised Transit

A simulated scenario under 50% Degradation: international capacity survives, but traffic is forced through upstream networks that the adversary controls (gateway failure kind k3, defined in §3.2.1, following the Kherson pattern). Measured by bandwidth, Compromised Transit looks like brownout, but what defines it is exposure to interception and lasting instability.

**What happens.** The adversary coerces gateway operators, physically or administratively, into routing international traffic through upstream ASNs that the adversary controls. Connectivity survives: traffic still reaches foreign destinations, and the BGP routes remain technically valid. All of that traffic, however, crosses the adversary's infrastructure, where it is exposed to content inspection, censorship and forced BGP path selection.

In Kherson, Ukraine, the instability took the form of a near-total outage on July 26, 2022. The outage came approximately two months after the first coerced routing change. The connection then remained unstable for more than a year ([Freedom House, Freedom on the Net 2022 Ukraine](https://freedomhouse.org/country/ukraine/freedom-net/2022)).

**Precedent.** In May 2022, Russian occupying forces in Kherson, Ukraine, coerced local ISP staff into moving their BGP upstreams to Miranda-Media (AS201776) in Crimea and on to Rostelecom. A BGP upstream is the network that carries an ISP's traffic to the rest of the internet. The network owner said they "had no choice", and Kentik's BGP analysis confirms the AS-path change ([Kentik, Rerouting of Kherson follows familiar gameplan, August 2022](https://www.kentik.com/blog/rerouting-of-kherson-follows-familiar-gameplan/)).

RIPE Labs measurements show that after the rerouting, latency to western networks rose and latency to Russian networks fell. Both latencies returned to their pre-occupation pattern immediately after liberation in November 2022 ([RIPE Labs, Resilience of the Internet in Ukraine, One Year On, April 2023](https://labs.ripe.net/author/emileaben/the-resilience-of-the-internet-in-ukraine-one-year-on/)).

**Applies to Taiwan:** partly. The route-capture technique works the same way as in Kherson and applies to any ISP whose physical gateway an adversary can reach. The geographic and political conditions differ.

**How to tell it from Multi-Cable Loss.** Compromised Transit changes the BGP AS path. On the affected international routes, route collectors (RIPE RIS or Routeviews) show transit ASNs controlled by the adversary where neutral carrier ASNs used to appear. Under Multi-Cable Loss, the AS paths of surviving routes do not change. The surviving cables carry traffic through their original peering relationships, and the loss is of capacity, not of routing topology.

**Drill note.** A tabletop exercise would test whether an engineering team recognizes the exposure to interception under Compromised Transit and tells it apart from a brownout that only reduces capacity, given three inputs: AS paths from route collectors that show the adversary's transit ASNs, degraded throughput and content-accessibility metrics, and no sign of cable faults or BGP withdrawals.
