## 2.2 Global Cutoff

### Definition

Global Cutoff is the rung at which the island loses 100% of its bandwidth to the global internet while the domestic network continues to operate normally: intra-island ISP links, domestic IXPs (TPIX/TWIX/STUIX), and locally hosted services. The international loss that 50% Degradation imposes at half scale reaches its terminal state here, with bandwidth to the global internet reduced to zero across all international cables. A service whose infrastructure is onshore and whose dependencies are domestically routed therefore operates normally, while a service with any offshore component or dependency becomes unreachable.

Global Cutoff does not imply domestic network failure, which is why the report does not call it a "total blackout"; a scenario in which the domestic network also fails has crossed into Island Partition.

### Failure texture

The failure texture of Global Cutoff is a hard edge: international traffic stops rather than slows. Where 50% Degradation produces brownout (HTTP 504 gateway timeouts, elevated packet loss, congestion driven by TCP retransmission), Global Cutoff produces connection timeouts at the TCP layer for every destination outside Taiwan's domestic network: the SYN packet receives no reply, and the attempt ends at the operating system's TCP timeout rather than at a slow acknowledgment. Domain Name System (DNS) queries for non-.tw names whose authoritative servers are off-island return SERVFAIL rather than delayed responses.

Two mechanically distinct events have produced this edge. Iran's administrative shutdown of November 2019 acted through BGP withdrawal at the gateway autonomous systems, with the cables physically intact, and kept data-plane connectivity low from 16 November until 21 November ([OONI, November 2019](https://ooni.org/post/2019-iran-internet-blackout/)). Tonga's cable loss of January 2022 produced the same edge through physical destruction, after which Cloudflare Radar recorded "close to no traffic at all" from Tonga's ISPs ([Cloudflare Blog, Tonga Internet Outage, 2022](https://blog.cloudflare.com/tonga-internet-outage/)). In both events the domestic network and domestically hosted services continued to function while every foreign-hosted service stopped.

### Time texture

The speed of onset depends on the mechanism. An administrative shutdown, by gateway route withdrawal or by application-layer allowlisting, has no brownout precursor and in network terms begins at minute zero; in Iran in 2019, cellular operators were disconnected first, at around 14:00 UTC on 16 November, and almost all other providers followed over the next five hours ([OONI, November 2019](https://ooni.org/post/2019-iran-internet-blackout/)). Physical cable severance is rapid but not instantaneous: BGP withdrawals propagate within minutes of physical link loss, and in Tonga Cloudflare Radar recorded near-zero traffic within 2.5 hours of the eruption's impact ([Cloudflare Blog, Tonga Internet Outage, 2022](https://blog.cloudflare.com/tonga-internet-outage/)).

Recovery also depends on the mechanism. Physical damage bounds it to an engineering schedule of repair-ship dispatch, splicing, and testing; Tonga, a single-cable country, reconnected its main island after 38 days ([Al Jazeera, February 2022](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2022/2/23/tonga-internet-reconnected-five-weeks-of-volcanic-eruption/)). An administrative cutoff has no engineering bound, because nothing is physically broken and no repair ship can end it; restoration is a political act. Documented cutoffs of the administrative gateway kind (k1) range from Iran's seven-day, protest-linked shutdown of 2019 ([OONI, November 2019](https://ooni.org/post/2019-iran-internet-blackout/)) to an Iranian shutdown that Al Jazeera described on April 5, 2026 as already record-breaking while it was still in force, and that Cloudflare reported as partially restored in May 2026 ([Al Jazeera, April 2026](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2026/4/5/frustration-grows-as-irans-wartime-internet-shutdown-breaks-grim-record/); [Cloudflare Blog, Iran Internet Partially Restored, May 2026](https://blog.cloudflare.com/iran-internet-partially-restored-may-2026/)). *(Inference from OONI November 2019 and Al Jazeera April 2026 documented shutdown durations and Cloudflare May 2026 partial-restoration data; not independently confirmed)* Economic pressure contributed in both cases but did not reliably force resolution on any predictable schedule.

A second Phase follows if the cutoff is sustained, whatever its mechanism, as time-bounded protocols expire on their own clocks. Cached DNS records lapse first, each when its time-to-live (TTL) runs out: short-TTL records within hours of onset, records served through content delivery networks (CDNs) within a day, medium-TTL platform records within two to three days, and mail-exchanger (MX) and name-server (NS) records within a week. Certificate renewal through offshore Automatic Certificate Management Environment (ACME) endpoints halts at onset, but each certificate fails only at its own expiry, days to weeks later, and DNS Security Extensions (DNSSEC) signature (RRSIG) records fail as their signing windows close. Protocol-Clock Cascade simulates this deferred wave.

### Recognition signatures

Global Cutoff is recognized by a cliff in international traffic while domestic traffic continues. The cliff separates it from the gradual slope of 50% Degradation; the continuing domestic traffic separates it from Island Partition, under which cross-partition domestic traffic also fails.

BGP feeds alone do not reveal the cliff, because three mechanisms produce the same collapse of the traffic plane with different routing behavior:

| Mechanism | BGP feeds | Collapse visible in | Recovery path |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cable-loss cliff (physical severance) | Simultaneous WITHDRAW messages within minutes of link loss; monitors fire, and routing-table observers and RIPE Atlas probes confirm the withdrawal | Cloudflare Radar, as a step-function drop toward near zero | Physical repair queue, bounded by ship dispatch and engineering work |
| Allowlisting stealth (administrative order) | Routes remain announced; monitors do not fire | Active probing (IODA) and traffic volume (Cloudflare Radar) only | Political reversal, phased by policy |
| Carrier-internal forwarding failure | Routes remain advertised; monitors do not fire | Active probing, with a response pattern distinct from allowlisting | Geographic batching |

Administrative route withdrawal, the Iran 2019 form, shows the signatures of the first row and is separated from cable loss only by its recovery path, which is political reversal.

Each row rests on a documented incident. In Tonga, the BGP update spike was recorded at 05:35 UTC on January 15, 2022 ([Cloudflare Blog, Tonga Internet Outage, 2022](https://blog.cloudflare.com/tonga-internet-outage/)). In Iran's January 2026 shutdown, 98.14% of Iranian IPv4 address space remained globally routed throughout, yet human-generated traffic fell to well under 1% of previous levels ([arXiv preprint 2603.28753, 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.28753v1); [Cloudflare Blog, Iran Internet Partially Restored, May 2026](https://blog.cloudflare.com/iran-internet-partially-restored-may-2026/)); IODA showed approximately 3% nominal responsiveness, representing allowlisted government-entity access, so an engineer monitoring only BGP feeds observed no event while users experienced a full cutoff. In Rogers Canada's July 2022 outage, approximately 240 IPv4 and 120 IPv6 routes remained advertised in Rogers' routing table for more than eight hours while traffic fell to near zero, because the failure lay in the internal forwarding plane rather than in the routing protocol ([Kentik, A Deeper Dive into the Rogers Outage, 2022](https://www.kentik.com/blog/a-deeper-dive-into-the-rogers-outage/)); the blind spot matches allowlisting stealth, but its cause is a failure rather than a policy.

DNS failures begin at onset and then accumulate. SERVFAIL rises immediately for domains with off-island authoritative servers when BGP withdrawal triggers the cutoff, and it can rise earlier when application-layer filtering precedes withdrawal, as in Iran in 2019, where OONI data show filtering leading withdrawal by approximately one hour. Meta's October 2021 outage (§3.3.1) documents the BGP-to-DNS step: withdrawal of the prefixes hosting Meta's DNS made recursive resolvers return SERVFAIL for Meta's domains at once ([Cloudflare Blog, October 4, 2021](https://blog.cloudflare.com/october-2021-facebook-outage/)). Cache staleness then grows on the TTL schedule given under Time texture.

### Simulated scenarios

#### Administrative Cutoff

A simulated scenario under Global Cutoff: the gateway operator, acting under government order, reduces international traffic to near zero by route withdrawal or by application-layer allowlisting (the k1 gateway kind) while the cables remain physically intact and the domestic network continues normally.

**Narrative shape.** The simulated event begins when a government order reaches the autonomous systems that carry Taiwan's international gateway traffic. In the route-withdrawal form (the Iran 2019 pattern), routing-table monitors fire within hours, and withdrawal by cellular operators ahead of fixed-line operators leaves a staggered signature in public BGP feed data ([OONI, November 2019](https://ooni.org/post/2019-iran-internet-blackout/)). In the allowlisting form (the Iran 2026 pattern), routes remain globally announced and only active-probing platforms and traffic-volume curves reveal the cutoff ([arXiv preprint 2603.28753, 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.28753v1)). Both forms end with international connectivity near zero over undamaged cables: onshore services without foreign-routed dependencies continue, every foreign-addressed endpoint becomes unreachable, and political and economic dynamics, not an engineering schedule, bound the duration.

**Precedent.** Iran's November 2019 shutdown is the precedent for the route-withdrawal form: orders to two state-controlled gateway entities, TIC (AS49666) and IPM (AS6736), propagated to all ISPs without per-ISP action and collapsed traffic by approximately 95% within hours while the cables were intact, and domestic banking, messaging, and mapping services remained fully operational throughout ([OONI, November 2019](https://ooni.org/post/2019-iran-internet-blackout/); [Kentik, From Stealth Blackout to Whitelisting, 2022](https://www.kentik.com/blog/from-stealth-blackout-to-whitelisting-inside-the-iranian-shutdown/)). Iran's January 2026 shutdown is the precedent for the allowlisting form at the same gateway infrastructure ([arXiv preprint 2603.28753, 2026](https://arxiv.org/html/2603.28753v1)). An earlier measurement study found the same split between routing-plane and data-plane shutdowns in 2011: Egypt's shutdown appeared in BGP data as the withdrawal of its prefixes, and Libya appears to have tested firewall-based blocking before moving to BGP-based disconnection ([Dainotti et al., ACM IMC, 2011](https://doi.org/10.1145/2068816.2068818)). Taiwan transferability: structural. Taiwan's international bandwidth is concentrated in two Chunghwa Telecom gateway networks, HiNet and TWGate (§3.2.1), the gateway concentration that a coordinated order would require. The political threshold is higher than under Iran's two-AS architecture: Taiwan has no sovereign-internet law and no standing authority for gateway route withdrawal comparable to Iran's Ministry of ICT order structure, so an administrative shutdown would require emergency telecommunications authority and carrier-level compliance.

**Recognition discriminator.** In its allowlisting form, Administrative Cutoff differs from All-Cable Loss on the BGP axis, because traffic collapses to near zero with no withdrawal for routing-table monitors to observe; in its route-withdrawal form, it shares the withdrawal cliff and differs in recovery, which follows policy reversal rather than a physical repair queue.

**Drill note.** A tabletop exercise that presents a simulated gateway order under which international traffic collapses without a BGP alert would test whether teams detect the cutoff through active-probing dashboards rather than routing-table monitors and identify which services still function on domestically routed traffic alone.

#### All-Cable Loss

A simulated scenario under Global Cutoff: physical damage severs every international submarine cable, leaving zero cable bandwidth to the global internet while the domestic network operates normally.

**Narrative shape.** The simulated event opens with the simultaneous or cascading severance of Taiwan's international cable plant, either by coordinated physical damage or by a seabed turbidity event of the geometry seen in the 2006 Hengchun earthquake (§2.1, Multi-Cable Loss). BGP withdrawals from the affected ASNs propagate within minutes and Cloudflare Radar records the cliff, while domestically hosted services continue. In a folded variant the damage also destroys cable landing stations, so terminal infrastructure must be rebuilt before cable repair can be completed. That variant shows the same BGP and traffic-plane signatures as cable-only severance, but the repair queue alone no longer bounds its recovery, and the recovery tail extends from weeks to months or longer, depending on which stations are destroyed and the scale of reconstruction.

**Precedent.** Tonga's January 2022 eruption, which severed the Tonga–Fiji cable, is the cleanest available measurement of a country losing its entire cable plant; its onset signatures and 38-day main-island recovery are given above ([Cloudflare Blog, Tonga Internet Outage, 2022](https://blog.cloudflare.com/tonga-internet-outage/); [Al Jazeera, February 2022](https://www.aljazeera.com/news/2022/2/23/tonga-internet-reconnected-five-weeks-of-volcanic-eruption/)). Taiwan transferability: moderate. Tonga had a single cable, whereas Taiwan had 15 international submarine cables at the end of February 2026 ([MODA press release, April 2026](https://moda.gov.tw/en/press/press-releases/19392)), with over 300 Tbps of designed capacity ([Ketagalan Media, February 2025](https://ketagalanmedia.com/2025/02/23/taiwans-submarine-cable-network-strategic-value-and-future-outlook/)). Physical damage alone would have to exceed the scale of the 2006 Hengchun earthquake, which severed eight systems but left surviving cables that prevented a complete cutoff, or coordinated damage would have to reach every active cable system. The multi-cable event after Taiwan's end-2025 earthquakes, which MODA rerouting absorbed (§2.1, Multi-Cable Loss; [MODA press release, December 2025](https://moda.gov.tw/en/press/press-releases/18412); [MODA press release, April 2026](https://moda.gov.tw/en/press/press-releases/19392)), places the threshold at which rerouting capacity is exhausted above the scale of moderate multi-cable loss.

**Recognition discriminator.** All-Cable Loss always opens with a BGP withdrawal cliff that routing-table monitors detect, which separates it from the allowlisting form of Administrative Cutoff, and its recovery is bounded by a physical repair queue that no policy reversal can shorten, which separates it from the route-withdrawal form.

**Drill note.** A tabletop exercise that presents the Cloudflare Radar and IODA signatures of a simultaneous multi-cable severance would test whether teams triage the incident from BGP withdrawal feeds and traffic-cliff data and identify which domestically hosted services remain reachable when physical repair timelines rule out any near-term restoration of international cables.

#### Satellite Trickle

A simulated scenario under Global Cutoff: cable bandwidth is gone and residual satellite capacity sustains critical-service traffic only, a trickle to triage rather than a network restored to general use.

**Narrative shape.** Satellite Trickle begins inside another Global Cutoff event, most naturally All-Cable Loss, once residual satellite paths become the only international link. Satellite capacity available over Taiwan is well under 0.1% of its cable bandwidth (§3.1.2), and no measured figure for its usable share in a cutoff has been published. Per-terminal LEO throughput is measured in tens of Mbps: MODA reports average speeds of up to 90 Mbps download for the OneWeb service its contingency network uses (§3.1.2), and crowdsourced measurements of Starlink, a different LEO system, put most clients at about 50–100 Mbps download at the 75th percentile in 19.2 million speed tests from 34 countries since 2021 ([Mohan et al., ACM Web Conference, 2024](https://doi.org/10.1145/3589334.3645328)). Priority allocation, analogous to Chunghwa Telecom's prioritization of critical circuits on Matsu's microwave backup during the 2023 cable loss (§2.1; [Taipei Times, February 10, 2023](https://www.taipeitimes.com/News/taiwan/archives/2023/02/10/2003794094/)), channels the trickle to emergency coordination, government communications, and prioritized financial transactions, and general civilian internet traffic does not cross it at functional speed, if at all. Taiwan's published plans describe the same triage. Chunghwa Telecom has said that if all of Taiwan's more than ten international cables are cut, high-, mid- and low-orbit satellites take over in layers, and the National Communications Commission's (NCC) wartime plan, as reported, cuts public 4G/5G capacity to 1% of normal and carries voice and SMS only (劉致昕, 管婺媛 and 陳泳丞, 〈戰火下的新商業〉, *商業周刊* no. 2029, October 5, 2026, pp. 84–85). The same report quotes 林一平 (Lin Yi-Bing) of National Yang Ming Chiao Tung University warning of signalling overload (「信令過載」), and notes that under disaster roaming a base station sized for 200 people serves 300. Chunghwa Telecom also puts its prepared capacity at about 50–60 times (五、六十倍) what basic communication (「基本通訊」) needs (劉致昕, 管婺媛 and 陳泳丞, 〈戰火下的新商業〉, *商業周刊* no. 2029, October 5, 2026, p. 84); that multiple is set against a basic-communication baseline rather than normal traffic, so it measures something different from the usable figure above.

**Precedent.** After Tonga's cable was severed in January 2022, Cloudflare Radar recorded only "minimal Internet traffic via satellite services" from Tonga's ISPs, a signal measurably above zero that came from individual satellite-terminal users rather than an activated national backup plan ([Cloudflare Blog, Tonga Internet Outage, 2022](https://blog.cloudflare.com/tonga-internet-outage/)). Tonga's 15-year satellite connectivity agreement with Kacific, signed in April 2019, had never been activated and was in arbitration in Singapore when the eruption cut the cable, and two days after the eruption Kacific said it was still awaiting the government's instructions to activate the service ([ZDNet, January 17, 2022](https://www.zdnet.com/article/kacific-waiting-for-tongan-contract-activation-to-supply-satellite-services-post-eruption/)). *(Structural inference from the unactivated Kacific agreement)* A signed satellite contract is therefore not an operational backup until it is activated. Ukraine's experience in 2022–24 corroborates the characterization of satellite capacity as a trickle rather than a network: Starlink's traffic share grew by over 1,600% from March to December 2022 and its daily users reached approximately 150,000 or more by January 2023, yet it never exceeded 0.3% of total Ukrainian internet traffic at any measured point, and its value lay in frontline and mission-critical contexts rather than in restoring general population traffic ([Cloudflare Blog, One Year of War in Ukraine, February 2023](https://blog.cloudflare.com/one-year-of-war-in-ukraine/)). Taiwan transferability: moderate. Taiwan has no pre-positioned national satellite backup, and LEO capacity over Taiwan is not sized for national fallback at anything approaching normal cable volumes. Starlink is not lawfully available to civilian users onshore, although the July 2026 amendment removed the foreign-ownership barrier (§3.1.2; [Focus Taiwan, July 21, 2026](https://focustaiwan.tw/business/202607210011)).

**Recognition discriminator.** Satellite Trickle leaves a thin but nonzero signal in Cloudflare Radar and active-probing data, a sliver above the zero baseline in which priority traffic categories survive disproportionately relative to general consumer traffic, whereas All-Cable Loss with no residual path collapses to near zero and leaves only noise-level signals.

**Drill note.** A tabletop exercise that presents a simulated bandwidth envelope calibrated to the trickle range would test prioritization judgment across traffic categories, asking which service categories survive at severely constrained international capacity rather than testing any path to full international connectivity.

#### Protocol-Clock Cascade

A simulated scenario under Global Cutoff: the cutoff persists into the expiry windows of time-bounded protocols (push channels, refresh tokens, software-license check-ins, certificate renewal, DNSSEC signatures, OCSP revocation freshness, ACME endpoint reachability, and analogous check-ins), producing a deferred failure wave distinct from onset failures.

**Narrative shape.** The simulated event begins after the first Phase of a Global Cutoff has settled, when services that survived the loss of connectivity appear to function, and a second wave of failures arrives hours to weeks later. Expiring certificates cannot renew because the ACME endpoint is offshore, and as RRSIG records reach the end of their signing windows, DNSSEC-validating resolvers return SERVFAIL for previously reachable names. OCSP staples go stale once a server's cached response passes the ten-day validity cap analyzed in §3.3.2 ([CA/Browser Forum Baseline Requirements 2.2.8, §4.9.9](https://cabforum.org/working-groups/server/baseline-requirements/documents/CA-Browser-Forum-TLS-BR-2.2.8.pdf)), after which clients that enforce hard-fail OCSP checking reject otherwise valid certificates. Push tokens, software-license check-ins, and refresh tokens expire on product-defined clocks. Because each protocol layer has its own expiry horizon, the wave accumulates over days and weeks.

**Precedent.** [IETF RFC 8555, March 2019](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc8555) specifies an ACME renewal model in which the client must reach a certificate authority (CA) endpoint to complete each renewal cycle, and [IETF RFC 4034, March 2005](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4034) and [IETF RFC 4035, March 2005](https://www.rfc-editor.org/rfc/rfc4035) specify RRSIG validity windows that expire unless authoritative infrastructure re-signs the records. The expiry of Let's Encrypt's DST Root CA X3 root on September 30, 2021, shows certificate-lifecycle failure under partial isolation (§3.3.2; [Let's Encrypt, DST Root CA X3 Expiration, September 2021](https://letsencrypt.org/docs/dst-root-ca-x3-expiration-september-2021/)). The IANIX DNSSEC outage list records signature failures across multiple incidents, among them the May 2026 key-rollover failure in the German .de TLD, which sent SERVFAIL to all DNSSEC-validating resolvers for millions of .de domains and shows how one failed validation step propagates to every resolution path that crosses it ([IANIX DNSSEC outage list](https://ianix.com/pub/dnssec-outages.html)). *(Structural inference from RFC 8555 ACME renewal model, RFC 4034 and RFC 4035 DNSSEC RRSIG validity windows, and the Let's Encrypt DST Root CA X3 expiry documentation cited above)* A sustained national cutoff would combine these expiry windows into a single deferred wave; no national-cutoff incident of this combined form is documented, so the simulated scenario is grounded in mechanism rather than in an observed incident. Taiwan transferability: strong for the mechanism. Taiwan's commercial services rely heavily on Let's Encrypt and other offshore ACME-issuing certificate authorities for TLS certificates, and DNSSEC signing for commercial .tw domains crosses offshore authoritative infrastructure; government domains under .gov.tw, by contrast, operate under the Government Root Certification Authority (GRCA, operated by DGBAS), an onshore certificate authority that continues to function under Global Cutoff.

**Recognition discriminator.** Protocol-Clock Cascade differs from All-Cable Loss on the time axis: All-Cable Loss fails at the moment of severance, with TCP timeouts and immediate SERVFAIL for off-island names, whereas Protocol-Clock Cascade fails days to weeks after onset, in a second wave that follows an initial stabilization and tracks certificate and DNSSEC expiry schedules rather than connectivity loss.

**Drill note.** A tabletop exercise that starts from a connectivity loss that has stabilized and steps the exercise clock forward in increments of days would test whether participants identify which protocol windows are expiring, which services are newly failing, and how each failure is observed, with no path back to international connectivity.
